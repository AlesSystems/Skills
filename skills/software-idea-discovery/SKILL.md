# Software Idea Discovery

Find a neglected problem a particular developer can solve and sell. Originality means a meaningful advantage for an underserved customer, grounded in evidence. The deliverable is a defensible opportunity and a cheap validation experiment.

## Interview the developer

Ask one focused question per turn and wait for the answer. Use earlier answers; adapt the next question to the biggest uncertainty instead of running a fixed questionnaire. Follow the user's preference if they request batches or a faster pass.

Establish:

- Preferred market, customer type, geography, and firsthand experience.
- Technical strengths, weekly hours, budget, team, and existing assets.
- Reachable customers, observed frustrations, and access to real workflows.
- Desired business model, income or adoption target, deadline, and risk tolerance.

Start with the preferred market when missing: “Which industry or community would you enjoy building for, and what experience do you have with it?” If undecided, explore their experience and customer access to suggest market directions before product ideas.

Summarize the working brief and mark assumptions. Begin market research once the market and customer are clear; fill other gaps through targeted questions as research progresses. Obtain missing goals and resource constraints before ranking opportunities. A fast pass uses explicit assumptions and provisional conclusions.

## Research the current market

Use available web search and browsing. Record the research date, source URLs, and publication or access dates. Inspect sources rather than citing search snippets. Match the chosen geography and segment.

Build a compact evidence table: **customer/job | current solution and price | unresolved pain | source/date | evidence strength**.

Inspect relevant direct competitors, adjacent products, and substitutes such as spreadsheets, agencies, and manual work. Use official features, documentation, pricing, and changelogs to establish existing coverage. Look for repeated problems in independent customer discussions, reviews, issue trackers, and practitioner accounts. Distinguish firsthand evidence, vendor claims, inference, and unknowns; complaints establish possible pain, not willingness to pay. Seek counterevidence, including failed products and reasons customers tolerate existing solutions. Syndicated claims count as one source.

Keep browsing bounded: examine the strongest plausible gaps and their closest substitutes. Broaden research only when an unanswered question could change the shortlist. If browsing is unavailable or declined, identify what cannot be verified, use dated user-provided evidence, and label the result provisional.

## Turn evidence into opportunities

First express each gap as **specific customer + costly or frequent job + failing workaround + reason alternatives underserve them**. Then derive a small shortlist, normally up to three distinct opportunities; fewer is valid. If evidence supports none, report that and identify the next research question.

For each opportunity explain:

- **Buyer and pain:** who uses it, who pays, how often the problem occurs, and evidence of urgency or spending.
- **Proposed advantage:** the narrow workflow solved, closest alternatives, and exactly what remains underserved. Check the advantage against current competitor capabilities. A new name, cosmetic change, or generic AI addition does not establish differentiation.
- **Developer fit and route to customers:** a feasible first version within the brief, access to the first buyers, switching barriers, and essential data or integrations.
- **Business viability:** pricing hypothesis, reachable demand, operating and support costs, and material regulatory or platform constraints. Separate assumptions from observed values.
- **Disconfirmation:** strongest counterevidence and the assumption most likely to invalidate the idea.

Novelty is relative to the researched segment and date. Describe the search coverage and differentiation confidence; never claim worldwide uniqueness from a limited search.

## Assess future success

Evaluate against the developer's defined target and horizon. Examine demand, payment evidence, differentiation, distribution, feasibility, economics, and durable advantage. Give a reasoned judgment and evidence confidence for each; avoid a weighted score that hides a fatal weakness.

Investigate relevant changes in technology, regulation, demographics, costs, or customer behavior using dated authoritative sources. Distinguish observed changes from projected adoption. Identify what makes the opportunity timely and what an incumbent, platform change, or commoditization could erase.

For finalists describe **downside, base, and upside scenarios**: explicit assumptions, observable leading indicators, failure conditions, and what would change the judgment. Use simple arithmetic where useful, such as target monthly revenue divided by assumed customer price, with costs considered separately. Numerical success probabilities require a relevant empirical basis and stated limitations; otherwise use qualitative judgments. Future scenarios are hypotheses, not promises.

## Finish with a testable decision

Present the brief, cited market evidence, opportunity comparison, and scenario assessment. Recommend the strongest opportunity to investigate, or explain why none clears the bar. Separate confidence in the evidence from potential attractiveness.

For the leading uncertainty, propose the smallest experiment with a **hypothesis, reachable participants, time/cost cap, observable commitment, and advance/stop threshold**. Favor actual workflow evidence and payment or pilot commitments over compliments. Draft interview questions or pilot materials; contacting people, collecting money, and building the product require a separate user request.

Ask the next question that could change the decision. Incorporate new answers and evidence into the brief and rerank when warranted. For skill maintenance only, use [the behavioral checks](references/behavioral-checks.md).
